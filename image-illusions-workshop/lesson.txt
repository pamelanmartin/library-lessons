SECTION: Lateral Reading

When you have any doubt about information you find online, use **lateral reading** to verify it. Instead of reading deeply into one source, you open new tabs and look at what *other* sources say about it.

Ask yourself these three questions:

CARDS:
CARD: 👤 Who's behind the information?
Who created this content, and what is their agenda or expertise?
CARD: 📄 What's the evidence?
Is the claim supported by data, research, or named sources?
CARD: 🔍 What do other sources say?
Do reputable outlets independently confirm this information?
END CARDS

TIP: What makes a source trustworthy?
Look for news and media outlets that follow clear journalistic standards and publish corrections when they make mistakes. Not every website that looks professional is reliable.
END TIP


QUIZ: Standards of Quality Journalism

The News Literacy Project lists seven standards that quality journalism follows. Read them, then answer the question.

- **Multiple Credible Sources:** eyewitness, official, expert
- **Verification:** the process of checking, confirming and supporting information and facts
- **Avoidance of Bias:** presenting the facts and context in a neutral manner
- **Balance:** representing multiple sides of the issue, event or controversy without giving unfair weight to one side or point of view
- **Documentation:** documents, data, reports, video, photos or audio
- **Context:** presenting the facts in a way that makes their meaning clear, fair and accurate
- **Fairness:** treating sources and subjects with appropriate respect, and giving subjects a chance to share their points of view or respond to any assertions or allegations about them

SECTION: Fact-checking Images

Images are one of the most powerful tools for spreading misinformation — they feel immediate, emotional, and real. But images can lie. Here are the most common techniques used to mislead people with images and videos online.

Note: memes use many of these same techniques, usually for humor. We may be more susceptible to image misinformation partly because we're so used to sharing memes without thinking twice.

CARDS:
CARD: 🔄 Out of Context
A real photo, used to illustrate a false claim. The image itself is genuine, but the caption or story around it is misleading.
CARD: ✂️ Manipulated Content
A real image that has been edited — cropped, spliced, colorized, or composited — to change its meaning. Includes deepfakes.
CARD: 🤖 AI-Generated / Fabricated
Entirely synthetic images created by tools like Midjourney, DALL-E, or Stable Diffusion. Nothing in the image is real.
CARD: 📸 Stock Images as "Reality"
Generic stock photos presented as if they show a specific event, place, or person that they don't actually depict.
CARD: 📐 Photographic Tricks
Forced perspective, unusual angles, or selective framing that make something look different from reality — no editing required.
END CARDS

HEADING: Spotting AI-Generated Images

Since 2023, AI image generators have become dramatically more realistic and widely available. The Hollywood sign “burning” in the LA wildfires, fake celebrity photos, fake news photos — all generated by AI and widely shared. Here's what to look for:

HEADING: Tell-tale signs of AI-generated images

CARDS:
CARD: 👋 Hands and fingers
AI still struggles with hands. Count the fingers: extra digits, fused fingers, or unnatural poses are common giveaways.
CARD: 😁 Teeth and mouths
Look for too-perfect rows of teeth, merged teeth, or unnaturally symmetrical smiles.
CARD: 👁️ Eyes and reflections
Light reflections in eyes are often inconsistent, asymmetrical, or physically impossible.
CARD: 🌫️ Backgrounds and edges
Backgrounds blur strangely around hair, details repeat themselves, or objects blend together unnaturally.
CARD: 🔤 Text in the image
AI-generated text in signs, shirts, or labels is often garbled, misspelled, or nonsensical.
CARD: 👂 Ears and jewelry
Ears are frequently asymmetrical or distorted, and earrings or accessories often look melted or wrong.
END CARDS

BOX: AI images are getting better all the time
The tell-tale signs above are getting harder to spot. Newer AI tools can draw convincing hands, clean text and realistic lighting, so an image can pass every one of these checks and still be fake. Treat these signs as clues, not proof.

**Further reading:** See how convincing today's AI images have become in this [New York Times interactive (Oct. 8, 2026)](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2026/10/08/technology/ai-generated-image-look-close.html). U of I students can get access to the Times through the library: [get New York Times access](https://libguides.uidaho.edu/nytimes).
END BOX

TIP: When in doubt, do a reverse image search
Even if you can spot some AI tells, the most reliable method is still to do a reverse image search—that is, search for the image to see if similar or identical versions turn up elsewhere, and investigate whether the image has been altered. You can run a reverse image search on sites like Google Images and TinEye. You'll learn more in the next section.
END TIP

SECTION: Reverse Image Search

A normal search starts with words. A **reverse image search** starts with the picture itself. You hand the search engine an image, and it hunts for visually similar images across the web — then shows you where else that image has appeared, and how far back it goes.

That's what makes it the single most useful image fact-checking move you can learn. It answers the two questions that matter most: *where did this image come from*, and *has it been changed?*

HEADING: Your three main tools

CARDS:
CARD: 🔎 Google Images
**Best all-around.** Search by image at images.google.com or right in Chrome. Huge index. Results sometimes include news articles or fact-checks about the image, but often you'll just see similar-looking pictures.
BUTTON: Google Images | https://images.google.com/
CARD: 🕰️ TinEye
**Best for edits.** Smaller index, but it sorts results by *oldest first* and *most changed* — which is exactly how you find an original photo and spot what was added.
BUTTON: TinEye | https://tineye.com/
CARD: 🔁 Bing Visual Search
**Worth a second look.** A different index than Google, so it sometimes finds matches the others miss. Useful as a cross-check when your first search comes up empty.
BUTTON: Bing Visual Search | https://www.bing.com/visualsearch
END CARDS

BOX: How to search an image you find online
1. **Right-click the image** (on a Mac trackpad, two-finger click; on a phone, press and hold).
2. In Chrome, choose **“Search image with Google.”** A side panel opens with matches. In Firefox or Safari, choose **“Copy Image Link”** or **“Copy image address”** instead.
3. If you copied a link, go to [TinEye](https://tineye.com/) or [Google Images](https://images.google.com/) and paste it into the search box.
4. You can also **save the image to your computer and upload it** — this always works, on any site, in any browser.

Browser menus change often. If you don't see these exact words, look for anything with the word “image” in it — saving and uploading the file is the reliable fallback.
END BOX

HEADING: When the fake is a person

A **deepfake** uses AI to make a real person appear to say or do something they never did. In this short film from MIT's Center for Advanced Virtuality, President Richard Nixon goes on TV in July 1969 to tell the nation that the Apollo 11 astronauts will never come home from the Moon.

Of course, they did come home. The video starts at Nixon's speech (4:41). Watch to the end, then answer the question.

QUESTION: Why was this video created?
TYPE: choice
- To convince people that the Moon landing was faked
- To confuse people with a misleading video
- To show the dangers of misinformation (correct)
- To teach historians about a speech the Nixon administration prepared but never delivered
RIGHT: Correct! MIT made this film to show how convincing deepfakes have become, and how easily they could be used to spread misinformation. The speech itself is real: Nixon's speechwriter, William Safire, wrote it in July 1969 in case the astronauts were stranded, but Nixon never had to give it. Real words, fake delivery.

TIP: A few things worth noticing
**The burden falls on the victim.** Proving a video of you is fake is far harder than making it. By the time a denial circulates, the clip has usually been seen far more widely.

**It doesn't have to be perfect to work.** A deepfake only needs to be convincing for the few seconds someone spends scrolling past it.

**The wider damage is doubt.** Once people know deepfakes exist, real recordings can be dismissed as fake too. That's sometimes called the “liar's dividend.”

**The habit that helps is the same one.** Slow down, check who posted it, and search for the original before you share.
END TIP

QUESTION: What agency did this image come from?

AFTER ANSWERS: What really happened
The source is real, but the “photo” isn't. It's a single frame from a NASA animation showing how Earth's magnetic field funnels particles from the Sun into the atmosphere, where they make the sky glow. PRACTICE: The 42-Pound Bullfrog

This photo has circulated online since 2012. Some versions are in black and white, which makes it look like an old snapshot. The caption claims this man caught a **42-pound bullfrog** in Arkansas. Could that be true?

IMAGE: bullfrog.jpg
ALT: A man standing by a pond holds a huge bullfrog out in front of him at arm's length. The frog looks nearly half his height.
CREDIT: 42-pound bullfrog: Photographer unknown; online since about 2012. Fact-check: [Snopes](https://www.snopes.com/42-lb-frog-photo/).

BOX: Investigate it
Use any reverse image search method you've learned, or search the text **“42 pound bullfrog.”**
END BOX

QUESTION: What old camera trick makes the frog look so big?
TYPE: choice
- Enlarging
- Polaroid
- Colorizing
- Forced perspective (correct)
RIGHT: Correct! It's forced perspective. The frog is held out toward the camera, so it looks much bigger than it is. Snopes estimates it weighed 1 to 2 pounds. A typical American bullfrog weighs about 1.5 pounds, and no frog on Earth comes close to 42.

AFTER ANSWERS: What is forced perspective?
Things closer to the camera look bigger than things farther away. Hold a small object near the lens and a person behind it, and the object looks huge. It's one of the oldest photo tricks there is, and it needs no editing at all.

That's why you can't “catch” this photo by looking for signs of Photoshop: nothing was changed. You have to think about the claim.

[See more forced-perspective fakes on Snopes ↗](https://www.snopes.com/list/forced-perspective/)
